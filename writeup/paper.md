## 1. Introduction + claim

Systems friction in civic digital services disadvantages outsiders; treating it as a first-class design requirement (not a post-launch fix) supports more accountable public digital services and public-interest AI over time. Madrid Spring 2026 made that friction legible in daily use across civic, university, commercial, and transit layers. Coursework supplied governance vocabulary; daily life supplied ground truth. This note does not claim legal expertise or generalizability beyond the stated method.

## 3. Method

Reflective practice and pattern extraction from lived systems use during one semester abroad, cross-checked against public ServeIT / Serve-AI clinic instincts (practice bridge only). Exploration vs confirmation kept separate in [`research-log.md`](./research-log.md). Public-safe notes only. Artifact: Outsider Impact Review six-check one-pager.

## 4. Findings / pattern library

Five patterns from existing Madrid docs (assumed local context; unwritten sequencing; language as access control; mismatched hours/channels; blaming errors), with concrete mechanisms (PDF-only English, phone-as-real-interface, wiped form state, mixed-language `lang` marking). Language: “appeared repeatedly / suggests,” not “proves.” Civic vs consumer apps: user did not choose the workflow.

## 6. Long-term AI growth (reflective, not forecast)

As AI tools proliferate over years: friction compounds when automation lands on a broken access baseline; “who the system assumes you are” can harden into proxies; Serve-AI community-scale rhyme (access alongside automation, human override, honest data scope); capability growth ≠ automatic inclusion (accessibility + staff capacity remain bottlenecks). Explicitly not a longitudinal study; no market sizes, % growth rates, or timelines as fact.
